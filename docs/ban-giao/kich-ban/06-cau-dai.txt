When the engineers finally opened the old vault, which had been sealed since the end of the war and which nobody in the building had dared to touch because of the rumors that it contained either a fortune or a disaster, they found, to their great surprise and to the quiet disappointment of the manager who had been waiting for this day for almost thirty years, nothing but a wooden chair, a dusty radio, and a handwritten list of names that none of them recognized.

The oldest worker in the room, a man who had spent his entire life repairing the pipes beneath the street and who rarely spoke more than a few words at a time, picked up the list, studied it carefully for a long moment, and then said in a voice that was barely louder than a whisper that these were the names of the families who had once lived in the houses that were torn down to build the factory.

For a while, nobody said anything, because the silence in that cold room seemed to carry more meaning than any speech could have, and because every person standing there understood that the real treasure in the vault was not made of gold but of memory.
