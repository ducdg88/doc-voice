Deep beneath the surface of the Pacific, where sunlight has never reached, the ocean floor is not empty. It is alive. Here, near cracks in the crust of the Earth, water heated to four hundred degrees Celsius pours out of chimneys of black rock. Around them, whole communities of strange animals thrive without a single ray of light.

Giant tube worms, some taller than a man, cluster around the vents. They have no mouth and no stomach. Instead, they carry billions of bacteria that turn poisonous chemicals into food. Crabs, pale and nearly blind, walk across the rocks. Shrimp swarm in clouds so thick that they hide the stone beneath.

Scientists first discovered these places in 1977, and the find changed biology forever. Life, it turned out, did not need the Sun. This raised a powerful question. If life can flourish in the crushing dark of the deep sea, could it also exist on other worlds? On Europa, a moon of Jupiter, a hidden ocean lies under miles of ice. It may have its own vents, its own heat, and perhaps its own strange creatures.

The next missions will carry cameras and sensors to look for answers. Until then, the vents remind us how little we know about our own planet. We have mapped the surface of Mars in greater detail than the floor of our own oceans. Every dive reveals something new, and every discovery makes the world feel larger, and more mysterious, than before.
