The rise and fall of Rome is one of the most studied stories in history, and yet it remains full of surprises. Legend says that the city was founded in 753 BC by Romulus. In truth, it began as a cluster of villages on seven hills beside the Tiber River. Over centuries, these villages merged, and a small kingdom turned into a republic around 509 BC.

The republic was strong because it was flexible. Citizens voted, senators debated, and two consuls shared power for one year at a time. Armies were made of farmers who went home after the campaign. Rome defeated its neighbors in Italy, then faced a far greater enemy: Carthage, a naval empire in North Africa. The Punic Wars lasted more than a century. In 216 BC, at Cannae, the general Hannibal destroyed a Roman army of some 70,000 men in a single afternoon. Rome did not surrender. It rebuilt, adapted, and finally won, destroying Carthage in 146 BC.

Victory brought wealth, but also trouble. Enormous estates replaced small farms. Poor citizens crowded into the city. Ambitious generals such as Marius, Sulla, Pompey, and Julius Caesar commanded armies loyal to them, not to the state. In 49 BC, Caesar crossed the Rubicon with a single legion, and civil war began. He won, became dictator, and was assassinated on March 15, 44 BC.

Out of the chaos rose his adopted son, Octavian, who became Augustus in 27 BC. He called himself the first citizen, but in practice he was the first emperor. For the next two hundred years, the empire enjoyed what historians call the Pax Romana, a period of peace and prosperity. Roads, aqueducts, harbors, and public baths spread from Britain to Syria. At its height, under the emperor Trajan in 117 AD, Rome ruled about 60 million people.

Then the cracks widened. Plague, inflation, civil wars, and pressure on the borders weakened the state. In the third century, there were more than twenty emperors in fifty years. Diocletian divided the empire into eastern and western halves. Constantine moved the capital to Constantinople in 330 AD and favored the new religion of Christianity.

The western half struggled on. In 410, the Visigoths under Alaric sacked the city of Rome itself, a shock that echoed across the world. In 455, the Vandals plundered it again. And in 476, a Germanic commander named Odoacer removed the last western emperor, a boy named Romulus Augustulus. Many historians use that year to mark the end of ancient Rome.

Yet Rome did not vanish. The eastern empire, which we call Byzantine, lasted until 1453. Roman law shaped the legal systems of Europe. Latin grew into French, Spanish, Italian, and Portuguese. Roman roads still guide modern highways. And whenever we speak of republics, senates, or citizens, we speak in the language of a city that began, long ago, as a few huts on a hill.

What can we learn from it all? Perhaps that no power lasts forever, but that ideas outlive the people who carry them. Rome fell, and still, in a thousand quiet ways, it stands.
